\begin{abstract}
We test whether bilingual pretraining leaves persistent language-conditioned geometry in a shared-language representation space. Across matched controls using the Nanotron \texttt{160m} width preset (12-layer, 768-hidden transformer blocks; with the 128{,}256-token vocabulary and untied embedding/output matrices this exports to \(\sim\)310M parameters), we separate language-exposure matching from compute matching, enforce overlap and no-overlap English partitions, and evaluate only on held-out cultural probes. We find that residual mismatch remains non-zero under every control. Critically, contextual axis divergence is consistently much larger than embedding-space axis divergence across EN+L2 comparisons for all eight L2 targets, and the same representation-dependent pattern holds across the full non-English set. This gap is not explained by the tested controls, including alignment-residual checks and shared-document overlap tests. Layer-wise probing shows divergence rises sharply through middle layers (around 4-6), then compresses at later layers while remaining far above embedding-space levels. These findings show that embedding-only agreement diagnostics systematically underestimate directional mismatch in this bilingual comparison setting, and that orthogonal alignment alone does not neutralize shared-language representation space geometry at the contextual level.\footnote{Code available here: \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/bli}.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig1.pdf}
\caption{Study design and evaluation protocol. We compare an English-only model with bilingual English+L2 models, align each pair using neutral anchor words, and then evaluate on a held-out set of 1000 cultural probes in two representation types: token embeddings and contextual hidden states. The central preview is that contextual mismatch remains much larger than embedding mismatch after alignment.}

Cross-lingual pretraining is now routine, but conclusions about shared-language representation-space behavior often rely on post hoc alignment snapshots that conflate multiple confounds. A common practice is to align two models and treat the remaining disagreement as technical alignment noise. That interpretation becomes fragile when models differ simultaneously in language mix, token exposure, and representation family, and when the alignment method is applied only to embedding matrices while evaluation draws on contextual states.
Figure~\ref{fig:idea-overview} summarizes our controlled comparison setup and the held-out probe evaluation protocol used throughout.

The core challenge is that existing evaluations conflate at least three factors. First, \emph{exposure}: a comparison may change both secondary-language presence and English token count, making it impossible to isolate the effect of language presence alone. Second, \emph{overlap}: shared English documents between runs can inflate apparent agreement by making the two models look more similar than the language-exposure difference would predict. Third, \emph{representation dependence}: directional semantic mismatch may appear strongly in contextual states while remaining muted in static embeddings, so alignment diagnostics that inspect only embedding-space geometry can miss the dominant signal.

Prior work on cross-lingual representation-space structure \citep{pires2019multilingual,libovicky2020language,chi2020finding} and on BLI alignment limits \citep{vulic2020we,sogaard2018limitations} has documented that languages occupy partially distinct regions even in shared multilingual representations, but has not jointly controlled for all three confounds above, and has not quantified the representation-family gap in this setting. Work on training data overlap \citep{dodge2021documenting,lee2022deduplicating} establishes that shared corpora inflate downstream agreement, but does not isolate this effect in controlled pretraining comparisons.

We address these gaps with a factorial design that holds architecture and optimization fixed. We run one EN-vs-bilingual comparison that matches English exposure and another that matches total optimization steps. We also compare shared versus disjoint English document partitions while keeping the rest of training fixed. Under each setting, we evaluate both embedding and contextual representations with the same alignment and probe protocol. Rather than aligning separately trained monolingual spaces (the standard BLI setup), we apply BLI evaluation \emph{within} a shared-language representation space across bilingual versus monolingual models. This inverted framing cleanly targets language-conditioned imprint rather than vocabulary mismatch.

We report four analyses to test these claims and rule out alternative explanations. All four use the same eight-language multilingual run matrix:
\squishlist
\item In \textbf{Experiment~1}, we test whether residual mismatch \footnote{We define \textit{residual mismatch} as non-zero disagreement on held-out probes after neutral-anchor alignment.} persists after neutral-anchor Procrustes alignment under strict exposure and compute controls, finding non-zero disagreement across all three metrics and all pairs.
\item In \textbf{Experiment~2}, we ablate whether shared English training documents explain the mismatch, finding a real but word-sparse overlap effect that does not account for most of the observed signal.
\item In \textbf{Experiment~3}, we quantify representation dependence and localize amplification across transformer depth via layer-wise probing, using the same multilingual settings as Experiments~1--2.
\item In \textbf{Experiment~4}, we compare orthogonal and affine alignment variants to test whether the contextual mismatch signal is robust to alignment-method choice.
\squishend
Together, these experiments separate persistence, overlap, representation family, and alignment-objective effects under one controlled training matrix. We use this structure to keep descriptive claims and causal scope aligned with what the controls directly test.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}
\paragraph{Bilingual lexicon induction.} Prior BLI work aligns monolingual spaces using orthogonal mappings and evaluates translation quality or neighbor consistency \citep{schonemann1966generalized,smith2017offline,conneau2018word,artetxe2018robust}. Later analyses show that geometric assumptions can fail under domain or frequency mismatch and that conclusions depend on evaluation criteria \citep{sogaard2018limitations,vulic2020we,patra2019bilingual}. A recurring finding is that orthogonal mapping quality degrades when embedding spaces are non-isometric, a failure mode attributed to typological and corpus-level divergences rather than method failure alone \citep{vulic2020we}. Our setting inverts standard BLI usage: rather than aligning separately trained monolingual spaces to measure translation quality, we align within a shared-language representation space across matched pretraining runs to measure language-conditioned residual geometry.

\paragraph{Multilingual representation structure.} Multilingual pretraining studies report both shared and language-specific internal structure \citep{pires2019multilingual,conneau2020unsupervised,libovicky2020language,chi2020finding}. Early work finds that mBERT generalizes cross-lingually without explicit alignment \citep{pires2019multilingual}, while subsequent analyses show that language-specific components persist even after joint multilingual training \citep{libovicky2020language}. Universal grammatical relations emerge across languages in middle layers, yet language-specific representation spaces remain active throughout the network \citep{chi2020finding}. Dufter and Sch\"{u}tze \citep{dufter2020identifying} identify shared vocabulary overlap as a primary driver of cross-lingual generalization, and recent work shows that large autoregressive models process non-English inputs through an English-dominant latent representation \citep{wendler2024llamas}, pointing to a deeper entanglement between primary and secondary language geometry than surface-level alignment would suggest.

\paragraph{Training data overlap.} Data-overlap work shows that shared documents inflate apparent cross-model agreement \citep{dodge2021documenting,lee2022deduplicating,muller2021unseen}. Large web corpora contain near-duplicate content that propagates into downstream model similarity in ways difficult to control post hoc \citep{dodge2021documenting}. Deduplication measurably changes memorization and generalization behavior \citep{lee2022deduplicating}, and vocabulary overlap between languages explains part of zero-shot cross-lingual transfer \citep{muller2021unseen}. We address this confound with deterministic document partitions that either share or fully exclude English training documents across compared runs.

\paragraph{Semantic axis probing and cultural dimensions.} Semantic-axis probing uses difference-of-means directions to measure how representations distribute along culturally interpretable dimensions \citep{bolukbasi2016man,caliskan2017semantics,garg2018word}. Projecting word vectors onto these directions reveals occupational and attitudinal stereotypes in embedding spaces, and the signal shifts systematically across historical corpora \citep{garg2018word}. More recent work applies structured probing to large pretrained models to measure stereotype consistency \citep{nadeem2021stereoset}, to audit semantic geometry at scale \citep{liang2023holistic}, and to detect cross-cultural value differences in multilingual models using probes derived from the World Values Survey and Hofstede's dimensions \citep{arora2023probing}. Cross-cultural NLP has emerged as a distinct subfield \citep{hershcovich2022challenges}, with benchmarks such as CrowS-Pairs \citep{nangia2020crows} targeting social biases in masked language models. The cultural dimensions we probe are grounded in Hofstede's six national-culture dimensions \citep{hofstede2001culture}, Schwartz's cultural value orientations \citep{schwartz2006theory}, the Inglehart-Welzel cultural map \citep{inglehart2005modernization}, and the GLOBE societal-culture scales \citep{house2004culture}. A key property of axis-projection diagnostics is their sensitivity to directional geometry that neighborhood-based metrics miss: two models with similar nearest-neighbor graphs can still differ substantially in axis-projection patterns. We adapt this technique to compare pairs of models rather than to measure individual model bias.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:study-design}

Our experiments compare matched runs under one alignment method and one probe protocol.

\subsection{Training details}
\label{sec:training}

\paragraph{Data}
The data we use for pretraining is from the BabyBabelLM benchmark \citep{jumelet2025babybabellm}. We study eight non-English target languages: Chinese (\texttt{zho}, \ZHtag), French (\texttt{fra}, \FRtag), Persian (\texttt{fas}, \FAStag), Dutch (\texttt{nld}, \NLDtag), Ukrainian (\texttt{ukr}, \UKRtag), Bulgarian (\texttt{bul}, \BULtag), Indonesian (\texttt{ind}, \INDtag), and German (\texttt{deu}, \DEUtag). These are all of the Tier $1$ languages from the benchmark with roughly $100$ million English-equivalent tokens.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters}
All pretrained models use the same Nanotron \texttt{160m} width preset (12 layers, hidden size 768, FFN 3072, 12 heads). With vocabulary size 128{,}256 and untied token embedding/output matrices, exported checkpoints contain approximately 310M parameters. We train with fixed throughput of $32{,}768$ tokens per optimization step (batch size $8$, gradient accumulation $8$, sequence length $512$) on a single A$100$ GPU. Under this setup, $1500$ steps correspond to approximately $50$M tokens, while $3000$ steps correspond to approximately $100$M tokens.
We did not run a hyperparameter sweep in this study: the training recipe was fixed in advance and held constant across all conditions to preserve controlled comparisons.

We evaluate two control conditions that isolate language exposure from total compute:
\squishlist
    \item \textbf{\RegimeOne.} \ENONLY{} short runs ($1500$ steps) are compared against bilingual $3000$-step runs that alternate English and L$2$ in equal blocks, yielding exactly $1500$ English steps and $1500$ L$2$ steps.
    \item \textbf{\RegimeTwo.} \ENONLY{} long runs ($3000$ steps) are compared against the same bilingual $3000$-step runs, matching the total number of optimization steps.
\squishend

For overlap ablations, we keep architecture, optimizer, token budget, and bilingual schedule fixed but vary the English data used:
\squishlist
    \item \textbf{\SetupAName.} The same English data is used by \ENONLY{} and bilingual models.
    \item \textbf{\SetupBName.} Completely disjoint English data is used by \ENONLY{} and bilingual models.
\squishend

\subsection{Probes}
\label{sec:probes}

Probe roles are separated by design. Neutral anchors are used only for estimating the Procrustes map, while culturally loaded probes and semantic axes are strictly held out for evaluation. This separation prevents the alignment objective from directly fitting the evaluated cultural vocabulary.

\paragraph{Neutral anchors ($3000$ words).} We sample $3000$ neutral anchors from high-frequency English words (top $50$k by corpus frequency), applying stop-word filtering, ASCII-only constraints, length bounds ($3$-$14$ characters), and ensure no overlap with cultural probes or axis endpoints so that the Procrustes rotation estimated on them does not directly fit the evaluated cultural vocabulary. Frequency-based anchor selection follows standard BLI practice \citep{conneau2018word,vulic2020we}.

\paragraph{Cultural probes ($1000$ terms).} We define a culturally loaded evaluation lexicon by sampling $1000$ terms with balanced category coverage across ten domains grounded in existing literature: \DomValues, \DomKinship, \DomReligion, \DomFood, \DomFestivals, \DomClothing, \DomSymbols, \DomGovernance, \DomIdentity, and \DomDaily. The category ontology is anchored in Human Relations Area Files (HRAF)-style ethnographic domains and overlaps with World Values Survey (WVS) and Schwartz value blocks \citep{inglehart2005modernization,schwartz2006theory}.

\paragraph{Semantic axes ($50$ pairs).} Our $50$ semantic axes operationalize dimensions from four established cross-cultural psychology frameworks: Hofstede's six national-culture dimensions \citep{hofstede2001culture}, Schwartz's cultural value orientations \citep{schwartz2006theory}, the Inglehart-Welzel Traditional-Secular and Survival-Self-Expression dimensions \citep{inglehart2005modernization}, and the GLOBE societal-culture scales \citep{house2004culture}. Axes are grouped into the same ten domains above and then sampled within category to avoid ad hoc axis choices and improve domain coverage. The full axis-to-framework mapping with citations is in Appendix~\ref{tab:appendix-axis-grounding}. All axes and probes were fixed before model training, alignment, and evaluations.

\paragraph{Negative controls ($100$ words).} We define a $100$-word negative-control set drawn from lexical domains with documented cross-linguistic stability: body-part terms, basic physical/mathematical vocabulary, and natural-kind terms. These overlap substantially with the Swadesh core-vocabulary paradigm \citep{swadesh1955towards}. This serves as a specificity stress test: lower divergence on this set would support category concentration in cultural probes, while comparable divergence would indicate a broader lexical/contextual effect and motivate conservative interpretation.

For the non-English languages, we translate probes with NLLB\footnote{Huggingface model id: facebook/nllb-200-distilled-600M}. We then compute automated quality estimation (QE) scores with COMETKiwi\footnote{Huggingface model id: Unbabel/wmt22-cometkiwi-da}, where QE means predicting translation quality without requiring human reference translations. We also run back-translation (translate the target-language output back into English) and compare it to the original probe term. Finally, we manually review low-quality or ambiguous cases and mark them for correction or exclusion.

\subsection{Alignment Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

For each model pair $(A,B)$, we represent the selected feature family (embedding matrix or pre-LM-head contextual state) as matrices $X^a, X^b \in \mathbb{R}^{V\times d}$, where $V$ is vocabulary size and $d$ is representation dimension. Let $\mathcal{N}$ be the index set of neutral-anchor words. Then $X^a_{\mathcal N}$ and $X^b_{\mathcal N}$ denote the submatrices restricted to anchor rows. We estimate an orthogonal map $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ with $W^\top W=I$ via:
\[
W^\star=\arg\min_{W^\top W=I}\|X^a_{\mathcal N}W-X^b_{\mathcal N}\|_F.
\]
Here $\|\cdot\|_F$ is the Frobenius norm. Residual mismatch is measured on held-out cultural probes $\mathcal C$ (size $|\mathcal C|$) via three metrics:

For the affine robustness variant used in Experiment~4, we replace the orthogonality constraint with:
\[
(W^\star,b^\star)=\arg\min_{W,b}\|X^a_{\mathcal N}W+\mathbf{1}b^\top-X^b_{\mathcal N}\|_F,
\]
where $b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is a translation vector and $\mathbf{1}$ is an all-ones column vector with $|\mathcal N|$ rows. We then evaluate the same held-out metrics below.

\squishlist
\item \textbf{Neighborhood disagreement}
\[
D_{NN}(w)=1-\frac{\left|\mathcal N_k^a(w)\cap\mathcal N_k^b(w)\right|}{\left|\mathcal N_k^a(w)\cup\mathcal N_k^b(w)\right|},
\]
for each probe word $w\in\mathcal C$, where $\mathcal N_k^a(w)$ and $\mathcal N_k^b(w)$ are the $k$-nearest-neighbor sets of $w$ in models $A$ and $B$, respectively. Values are in $[0,1]$.
\item \textbf{Structural disagreement}
\[
D_{Struct}=\frac{\left\|S_a-S_b\right\|_F}{|\mathcal C|},
\]
where $S_a,S_b\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal C|\times|\mathcal C|}$ are cosine-similarity matrices over probe words in $\mathcal C$ for models $A$ and $B$: each entry $(i,j)$ is the cosine similarity between probe word $i$ and probe word $j$.
\item \textbf{Axis projection disagreement}
\[
D_{Axis,i}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal C|}
\sum_{w\in\mathcal C}
\left|\left\langle x_w^a,\hat u_i^a\right\rangle-\left\langle x_w^b,\hat u_i^b\right\rangle\right|,
\]
for axis $i$, where $x_w^a$ and $x_w^b$ are the vectors of probe word $w$ in models $A$ and $B$. Axis directions ($\hat u_i^a, \hat u_i^b$) are unit vectors from axis endpoint words in each model. The notation $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ denotes the Euclidean inner product.
\squishend

We also record signed projection differences for interpretability. Unlike $D_{NN}$, axis divergence is not intrinsically bounded; in EN-null-centered analyses, embedding $\Delta D_{Axis}$ values cluster near zero while contextual $\Delta D_{Axis}$ values remain larger in most pairs. We therefore present absolute centered values in the main text and keep ratio summaries as appendix normalization checks.

For EN representation-space analyses, we center the three core metrics by EN-only seed-null baselines: for checkpoint $c\in\{50\text{M},100\text{M}\}$, representation family $r$, and metric $m$, we report $\Delta m = m - \bar m_{\text{EN-null}}(c,r,m)$, where $\bar m_{\text{EN-null}}$ is the mean over the three EN-seed pairings. Positive values indicate above-null mismatch and negative values indicate below-null mismatch.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

We report four experiments that test persistence, overlap effects, representation dependence, and alignment-method robustness.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Residual Mismatch Under Exposure and Compute Controls}
\label{sec:exp1}
This experiment tests whether neutral-anchor alignment collapses disagreement under strict controls.

\textbf{Setup.} We evaluate sixteen EN-centered pairs formed by crossing two \ENONLY{} baselines (EN-50M under \RegimeOne; EN-100M under \RegimeTwo) with all eight bilingual families (EN+L2), all under \SetupAName. For each pair, we estimate orthogonal Procrustes on 3000 neutral anchors and evaluate on 1000 held-out cultural probes across three metrics. Both embedding matrix and pre-LM-head contextual representations are evaluated under the same alignment.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} \textbf{(H1.1)} If alignment is sufficient, all three disagreements should be approximately zero. \textbf{(H1.2)} If residual mismatch is systematic, all pairs should retain non-zero disagreement. \textbf{(H1.3)} Contextual axes should show lower agreement than embedding axes.

Each language is shown as a pastel bar with a language-specific hatch pattern; whiskers are 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals. The gray EN-null bar is fixed at zero with min--max whiskers from the three EN-only seeds. Most languages remain above zero, showing persistent mismatch after alignment.}

\textbf{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:exp1-controls} shows the contextual EN-50M primary setting directly in EN-null-centered units: $\Delta D_{Axis}$ remains non-zero across languages, with most language pairs above the zero-centered null reference. In the aggregate negative-control summary, disagreement is comparable to or higher than the cultural-probe summary (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:exp1-negctrl}), so this block weakens cultural-specific interpretation and is treated as a lexical-stability bound.

\textbf{Takeaway 1:} Orthogonal alignment improves shared structure but does not remove persistent residual mismatch.

\subsection{Experiment 2: English Overlap Ablations}
\label{sec:exp2}
This experiment isolates the effect of shared EN documents by holding language exposure and alignment fixed while varying document overlap.

\textbf{Setup.} For each bilingual family (EN+L2 across all eight L2 targets), we compare \SetupAName against \SetupBName, keeping bilingual EN exposure, architecture, and alignment identical. We report per-metric agreement deltas ($\Delta = C3 - C4$) and per-word Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over 1000 cultural probes.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} \textbf{(H2.1)} \SetupAName should increase agreement relative to \SetupBName. \textbf{(H2.2)} \SetupBName should still retain non-zero disagreement.

\textbf{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:exp2-overlap} shows the contextual EN-50M $D_{Axis}$ primary setting centered on the EN-null reference: most C3/C4 language points lie near each other and near the null band, indicating a generally small but heterogeneous overlap effect in this setting. Wilcoxon tests and full baseline/representation deltas (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:exp2-wilcoxon} and Table~\ref{tab:exp2-overlap}) detect effects in many settings with near-zero medians in most cases, alongside occasional larger pair-level deltas. Quality-tier stratification (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:appendix-exp2-quality}) gives the same pattern. \SetupBName still shows non-zero mismatch, supporting \textbf{(H2.2)}.

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Takeaway 2:} Shared documents produce a real but small and word-sparse overlap effect. Under these controls, language co-training is a more plausible contributor than shared corpora.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{Experiment 3: Representation Dependence}
\label{sec:exp3}
This experiment quantifies representation dependence under a single alignment protocol.

\textbf{Setup.} Using the same Procrustes alignment from Experiment~1, we extract two representation families under identical alignment: (1) embedding matrix (static token vectors) and (2) pre-LM-head contextual states (final hidden states after all transformer layers). We compute $D_{Axis}$ over the multilingual model set used in Experiments~1--2, including all sixteen EN-centered pairs and the non-English target-language pairs for all eight targets (\ZHtag, \FRtag, \FAStag, \NLDtag, \UKRtag, \BULtag, \INDtag, \DEUtag) at both 50M and 100M. For layer-wise localization, we extract hidden states from all 12 transformer layers and compute $D_{Axis}$ per layer.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} \textbf{(H3.1)} Contextual axis divergence exceeds embedding axis divergence across all language settings. \textbf{(H3.2)} The same amplification persists under both controls and across layer depth.

\textbf{Takeaway 3:} Representation choice dominates measured mismatch: in the EN-centered primary setting, contextual $\Delta D_{Axis}$ remains in a sub-unit range (about 0.054--0.940) while embedding $\Delta D_{Axis}$ stays near zero/tenths (about -0.005--0.036). Divergence rises through middle layers, peaks, then compresses while remaining elevated at final layers; full ratio summaries are reported in the appendix.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{Experiment 4: Alignment-Method Robustness}
\label{sec:exp4}
This experiment tests whether the main contextual mismatch signal is an artifact of one alignment objective.

\textbf{Setup.} We evaluate the same EN-centered C3 pairs from Experiment~1 under two effective alignment-objective families: orthogonal and affine. The main-text figure compares contextual-evaluation outputs under orthogonal versus affine alignment (both using embedding-anchor estimation for method comparability). We still compute orthogonal contextual-anchor alignment as a sanity-check variant and report the full three-variant matrix in Appendix/Table outputs.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} \textbf{(H4.1)} If the observed contextual mismatch is mainly a method artifact, at least one alignment variant should collapse the contextual axis gap. \textbf{(H4.2)} If the signal is representation-intrinsic, the gap should persist across variants.

\textbf{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:exp5-alignment} supports \textbf{(H4.2)} in the contextual EN-50M primary setting: switching from orthogonal to affine objective changes values, but does not collapse contextual $D_{Axis}$ across languages. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:exp5-alignment} provides full per-metric summaries across representation families and both baselines, including the contextual-anchor orthogonal sanity variant. Two additional robustness checks are consistent with this interpretation: Appendix Table~\ref{tab:same-language-controls} reports EN-only seed-matched null controls (same data, same budget, different seeds), and Appendix Table~\ref{tab:framework-holdout} reports axis-grounding holdout results where held-out axis sets continue to show elevated contextual mismatch. EN-only seed controls show contextual $D_{Axis}$ 2.115--3.154 (mean 2.610) and ratio 18.5--20.9. In the EN-null-centered EN comparisons, contextual $\Delta D_{Axis}$ spans 0.054--0.940 while embedding $\Delta D_{Axis}$ stays near zero (-0.005--0.036) (Figure~\ref{fig:exp4-ratio}; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:exp4-ratio}). Because centered embedding denominators can be close to zero, ratio ranges are descriptive only and we rely on absolute contextual mismatch plus controls for interpretation.

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Takeaway 4:} The contextual mismatch signal is robust to alignment objective choice; better anchor fit does not imply neutralized contextual geometry.
\end{insightbox}
\vspace{-3pt}

\paragraph{Category stratification.} Appendix~\ref{fig:appendix-category-heatmap} reports category-stratified contextual axis divergence. The signal is concentrated in food, color symbolism, and social-structure categories. Color symbolism axes (e.g., \texttt{white}$\rightarrow$\texttt{red}) show the largest per-axis divergence across EN-centered pairs, consistent with well-documented cross-linguistic variation in color semantics. Food and kinship terms show moderate but consistent elevation, while physical and mathematical terms, which substantially overlap with the negative-control set, are nearer that baseline and do not form the high-divergence contextual $D_{Axis}$ tail. We interpret this as category concentration in specific domains, while noting that aggregate negative-control mismatch remains comparable in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:exp1-negctrl}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\FloatBarrier

The control stack isolates complementary failure modes: exposure/compute controls show persistence under matched budgets, overlap controls show shared English documents are not the dominant driver in this setup, and alignment-method variants show contextual mismatch is not removed by changing the objective.

For multilingual auditing, a practical minimum is to report one EN-centered view, one multilingual L2-centered view, and one method-robustness view together (Figures~\ref{fig:exp1-controls}, \ref{fig:multilingual-combined}, \ref{fig:exp5-alignment}), while explicitly disclosing the remaining bounds from aggregate negative controls, EN-only seed-null overlap, limited 50M/100M checkpoint sensitivity, and translation-quality variability (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:appendix-exp2-quality}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Under strict exposure and overlap controls, bilingual pretraining leaves measurable residual language-conditioned geometry in a shared-language representation space after orthogonal alignment. Across tested settings, directional axis divergence is substantially larger in contextual than in embedding-space representations, with layer-wise localization and concentration in selected probe categories. We interpret this primarily as representation-family sensitivity of residual mismatch under our probe design, with cultural-specific claims limited by negative-control and seed-null overlap.

\newpage
\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

This study establishes a controlled baseline and opens four immediate extension directions: (1) scaling the same matched-control design to larger model sizes and additional architectures (including encoder-only and instruction-tuned variants) beyond the current Nanotron \texttt{160m} width preset (\(\sim\)310M exported parameters in this setup), eight-language Tier-1 BabyBabelLM setting; (2) broadening robustness with denser checkpoint trajectories and larger EN-only seed sets beyond the current 50M/100M and three-seed null protocol; (3) expanding probe coverage and translation quality, especially for political/legal/economic domains and harder multilingual lexical-equivalence cases; and (4) moving from measurement to intervention by adding causal tests and richer alignment objectives beyond orthogonal Procrustes, including affine or contextual-space objectives \citep{artetxe2019richly}. These directions are intentionally out of scope for the present evaluation-focused study and define the most promising next steps.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethical}

This work studies structural properties of bilingual language model representations and has no direct application to individual users or downstream production systems. Its primary broader impact is methodological: by demonstrating that embedding-space BLI diagnostics can substantially underestimate contextual-level language imprint (often by order-of-magnitude factors), the framework introduced here (held-out cultural probes, neutral-anchor alignment separation, and layer-wise analysis) can improve the rigor of fairness and safety audits in multilingual NLP systems. Models that appear cross-lingually neutral at the embedding level may carry culturally conditioned directional signals at contextual depth; surfacing this gap is a step toward more transparent evaluation practices. No personally identifiable or sensitive individual data were used. All probe translation was performed with automatic quality control and manual review at an aggregate vocabulary level, with full audit logs included in the released code and data artifacts.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter search policy and reported values}
This study does not use hyperparameter search.
